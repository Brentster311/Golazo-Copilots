## What Is a Bowler Chart?

A bowler chart is a performance tracking tool borrowed from bowling scorecards. The name comes from the visual shape — it reads like a bowling lane score sheet, left to right across time.

**Basic structure:**

Rows are metrics. Columns are time periods (weeks or months). Each cell shows actual vs. target. The chart is read left to right — past performance on the left, future commitments on the right — so trajectory is visible at a glance.

A typical bowler has three rows per metric:
- **Plan** — what was committed
- **Actual** — what happened
- **Variance** — the gap, color-coded green/yellow/red

**Why "bowler":**

Bowling scorecards track cumulative score frame by frame. A bowler chart does the same — it accumulates actuals period by period against a committed curve, so you see whether you're ahead or behind *cumulatively*, not just in the current period. Missing week 3 but recovering in week 4 looks different on a bowler than two separate numbers would suggest.

**Why Tim uses this:**

It directly operationalizes "narrative without evidence is not progress." You cannot say "we're on track" if the chart is red. The data speaks before anyone does. It also enforces named-lead accountability — each row is implicitly owned by someone, and the gap is visible to the entire room simultaneously.

## How a Bowler Differs From Other Agile Measurement Standards

The key differences are in philosophy of time, cumulative vs. point-in-time view, and who the audience is.

**Agile velocity (story points per sprint)**
Measures team throughput in a single sprint window. It resets each sprint — a bad sprint doesn't carry forward. A bowler accumulates. Missing your target in week 3 is still visible in week 12. Velocity also measures *effort processed*, not outcomes. A bowler can measure anything, including outcomes.

**Burn-down / burn-up charts**
Scoped to a single release or sprint. They answer "will we finish this thing by the deadline?" and then disappear when the thing is done. This is a finite-game tool. A bowler is continuous — there is no completion event that ends it. It runs indefinitely, which fits Tim's infinite game framing directly.

**OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)**
Quarterly goal-setting with 0–1.0 scoring at the end of the quarter. OKRs are evaluated episodically, not continuously. The bowler shows trajectory *during* the period, not just the outcome at the end. OKRs also tolerate ambiguity in how you get there. A bowler demands a committed curve — you have to say what the number will be in each period, not just what winning looks like at the end.

**DORA metrics (deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, mean time to restore)**
Engineering system health metrics that measure the technical delivery capability. A bowler is agnostic to what the rows contain — DORA metrics *could* go in a bowler, which is actually the strongest version of the tool. But DORA as typically implemented is a dashboard, not a commitment instrument. Nobody signs their name to a DORA target the way a bowler demands.

**NPS / customer satisfaction**
Outcome metrics measured infrequently (monthly or quarterly surveys) and often unconnected to specific delivery decisions. A bowler runs at weekly or monthly cadence against committed targets. The accountability discipline is tighter.

**The real difference, cut simply:**

Most agile metrics are *descriptive* — they tell you what happened. A bowler is *committal* — someone said this number would happen, and now the room can see whether it did. The psychological and accountability mechanics are completely different.

That's why Tim's model reaches for it. It's not a better measurement tool. It's a better *accountability* tool dressed up as a measurement tool. The chart is almost secondary — the primary function is that someone had to write a number in the future column before the future arrived.
