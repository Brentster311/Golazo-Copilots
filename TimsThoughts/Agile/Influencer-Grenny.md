# Influencer: The New Science of Leading Change — Applied to Tim's Delivery Direction

*Grenny, J., Patterson, K., Maxfield, D., McMillan, R., & Switzler, A. (2013). Influencer: The new science of leading change (2nd ed.). McGraw-Hill.*

*The Influencer framework is built around one empirically grounded claim: most change efforts fail because they rely on only one or two sources of influence. Lasting behavior change requires simultaneously engaging all six. The book identifies the six sources across two dimensions — motivation and ability — at three levels: personal, social, and structural.*

---

## The Six Sources of Influence

| | Motivation (Will They?) | Ability (Can They?) |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal** | 1. Make the undesirable desirable | 2. Surpass your limits |
| **Social** | 3. Harness peer pressure | 4. Find strength in numbers |
| **Structural** | 5. Design rewards and demand accountability | 6. Change the environment |

The authors call this **overdetermination**: stacking all six sources simultaneously so that the right behavior becomes the path of least resistance, socially expected, personally meaningful, and structurally supported. Any strategy that engages fewer than four sources reliably will underperform or fail.

---

## 1. Their First Question: What Are the Vital Behaviors?

Before the Influencer authors evaluate Tim's model, they would insist on a prior step Tim has not yet taken explicitly:

**Identify the two or three vital behaviors.**

Influencer distinguishes between the behaviors an organization wants to change (many) and the vital behaviors — the specific, observable, high-leverage actions that, if changed reliably, produce the desired results.

Vital behaviors are not values, principles, or cultural aspirations. They are:

- Observable (you can watch someone do or not do them)
- Specific (not "be accountable" but "put your name on a plan before work begins")
- High-leverage (changing this one behavior moves the whole system)

Tim's documents enumerate many behaviors across many dimensions. The Influencer authors would push hard on one question:

*If you could change only two behaviors across your leadership population, which two would produce the largest improvement in delivery outcomes?*

Their prediction: Tim could get 80% of his intended change by reliably shifting two or three vital behaviors. The whole corpus of documents is the scaffolding around those two or three things. Until those are identified explicitly, the change program risks dispersing effort across too many fronts simultaneously.

Their likely candidates from Tim's documents:

- **Named-lead accountability at the moment of commitment** — does a named human take ownership before work begins, or after ambiguity has already accumulated?
- **Escalation at first discovery** — does a team surface a blocker the moment it is known, or absorb it until crisis?
- **Evidence-first progress reporting** — does a leader present working proof before narrative, or narrative with working proof as an afterthought?

These three behaviors, reliably changed, would produce most of what Tim is asking for. Everything else is context.

## 2. Source 1 — Personal Motivation: Make the Undesirable Desirable

**Tim's current approach:** Assumes urgency is self-evident. "Delivery is existential" is the motivation statement. The trust is that leaders who understand the competitive and regulatory landscape will feel the stakes and act accordingly.

**Influencer's critique:**

Telling people the situation is urgent is not the same as helping them *experience* the cost of current behavior.

The Influencer authors use the concept of **vicarious experience** — allowing people to see the real consequences of the current state, often through stories or direct exposure to the results, before asking them to change. Rational arguments about AI competitiveness rarely shift motivation. Concrete, vivid, emotionally real experiences of what the current state costs — in human terms, not economic terms — reliably do.

Tim's documents are written for people who already believe delivery is existential. The Fireside Chat on April 16 (Start with Why, with guest speakers) is the closest the offsite gets to this source. But it is allocated 45 minutes at the start of the day and then the day moves to frameworks and metrics.

**Their recommendation:**

Design a personal motivation experience early in the change program — before the frameworks arrive. This might be:

- A team that failed to deliver a critical outcome describing what that failure cost in terms that hit the room personally
- A customer or partner describing what the delivery gap meant to them in real terms
- A "bright spot" team describing what changing their behavior actually felt like from the inside

The goal is to make the cost of the *old* behavior as concrete and vivid as the aspiration of the new behavior.

## 3. Source 2 — Personal Ability: Surpass Your Limits

**Tim's current approach:** Largely absent. Tim's documents assume leaders and engineers have the skills required to execute the model. The AWARE framework addresses AI skill development at the workforce level, but it is staged transformation, not immediate personal capability investment.

**Influencer's critique:**

Motivation without ability produces anxiety, not change. People who understand what is expected and believe it matters — but do not know how to do it — will default to the behaviors they already know.

The Influencer authors document this as one of the most common failure modes in organizational change programs: leaders are persuaded it matters, they commit publicly, and then they revert because they do not actually know how to do the new thing.

Specific skill gaps the authors would identify in Tim's model:

- **Escalation** — "escalate early" sounds simple. In practice, escalating to a senior leader is psychologically costly and requires specific conversational skills (Crucial Conversations, from the same author group). Many leaders will absorb organizational pain rather than have a conversation they do not know how to navigate.
- **Writing clarity into artifacts** — the PM-as-clarity-engine role requires skill in distilling ambiguity into unambiguous written language. This is a learnable craft. It is not innate.
- **Running a steel thread** — composing a vertically integrated increment across team boundaries requires coordination skills that most teams have not practiced.

**Their recommendation:**

Pair each vital behavior with a deliberate practice structure: a safe environment where the new skill can be rehearsed with coaching before it is performed under real stakes. This is not training in the conventional sense — it is structured practice with expert feedback at the moment of application.

## 4. Source 3 — Social Motivation: Harness Peer Pressure

**Tim's current approach:** Harambee is Tim's richest engagement with social motivation. The concept of collective effort, synchronized force, and team identity is directly in this source. Mission teams as a social unit also contribute — small teams develop peer accountability naturally.

**Influencer's assessment:** This is Tim's strongest source engagement, and it is genuinely powerful. The Influencer authors would validate the Harambee framing strongly. Social norms within a small team are among the most reliable behavioral regulators known. A mission team where everyone visibly produces weekly evidence of progress creates strong social pressure for each individual to do the same.

**The gap they would identify:**

**Opinion leaders.** The Influencer authors consistently find that a small number of people in any social network have disproportionate influence over what others believe and do. These are not necessarily the senior leaders — often they are mid-level respected practitioners whose judgment others trust.

Tim's model is organized around formal authority (named leads, escalation chains). Influencer would ask:

*Who are the informal opinion leaders in this organization, and are they modeling the right behaviors?*

If the opinion leaders — the engineers others watch to decide what is really expected — are not visibly embodying the vital behaviors, the social norm will not shift regardless of what the formal authority says.

The April 16 offsite is a formal authority event. The guest speakers (Zia Mansoor, Andy Mutz, Pranav Mehra, Kristin Chester) are presumably formal leaders. Influencer would want to know: who are the informal opinion leaders watching from the back row, and what are they thinking?

**Their recommendation:**

Identify six to ten informal opinion leaders across the affected organization. Invest in them first — not in cascade training, but in direct engagement with the vital behaviors and the reasoning behind them. When informal opinion leaders model the change, the social norm shifts faster than any top-down communication program can achieve.

## 5. Source 4 — Social Ability: Find Strength in Numbers

**Tim's current approach:** Partially addressed by mission team composition. Cross-functional teams provide adjacent expertise. Open contribution across boundaries provides some knowledge-sharing surface.

**Influencer's critique:**

Composition is not the same as mutual support. A mission team in which each member has the skills needed is Source 2. A mission team in which members actively help each other develop the skills needed is Source 4.

The Influencer authors describe **social capital** as a critical delivery resource — the accumulated trust, reciprocity, and expertise-sharing that allows a team to solve problems it could not solve individually. This is not a cultural aspiration. It is a specific set of behaviors: asking for help visibly, teaching rather than just doing, sharing failure stories as learning inputs.

Tim's model includes open contribution (contribute across boundaries), which is a structural enabler. But it does not explicitly design for mutual skill development within mission teams.

**Their recommendation:**

Build explicit knowledge-sharing rituals into mission team rhythms — not retrospectives (which are episodic), but brief, frequent practices: who learned something this week? who is stuck on something others might help with? These practices are Source 4 in action. They also serve as an early warning system for the skill gaps Source 2 is trying to address.

## 6. Source 5 — Structural Motivation: Design Rewards and Demand Accountability

**Tim's current approach:** This is Tim's dominant source. The bowler chart, weekly proof of progress, named leads, earned headcount, escalation discipline — these are all Source 5.

**Influencer's assessment:**

Source 5 is necessary and Tim has developed it more carefully than any other source. The bowler chart in particular is a textbook Source 5 instrument: visible, continuous, committal data that makes accountability inescapable without requiring a conversation.

**The risk the authors would flag:**

When Source 5 is dramatically stronger than Sources 1-4, the change system produces **compliance policing, not culture change.** People learn to perform the right behaviors in visible contexts and revert in invisible ones. The bowler gets gamed. Weekly evidence reporting becomes narrative with thin evidence attached. Named leads learn to manage accountability through pre-framing rather than through genuine delivery.

This is not a failure of the accountability mechanism. It is what happens when accountability exists in the absence of personal motivation, skill, social modeling, and peer support.

Their empirical finding:

Organizations that rely primarily on Source 5 get short-term compliance gains followed by reversion and cynicism. Organizations that stack Sources 1-4 alongside Source 5 get durable behavior change.

The Influencer authors would describe Tim's April 16 agenda as a Source 5 event. The bowler session (3.5 hours) is all Source 5. The commitment close (50 minutes) is Source 5. The mindset session is an attempt at Source 1 but is unlikely to produce vicarious experience in 60 minutes on a Friday afternoon.

## 7. Source 6 — Structural Ability: Change the Environment

**Tim's current approach:** Partially developed. The toil-reduction mandate, platform primitives, AI tools, reusable components, and single plan of record are all Source 6 — removing environmental friction that makes the right behavior harder than the wrong behavior.

**Influencer's assessment:** This is Tim's second-strongest source and the one most aligned with the Lean/Shalloway criticism in this folder. Environmental design is more reliable than motivation design because it does not require willpower. If the environment makes escalation easy (clear channel, expected pattern, no social penalty), escalation will happen. If the environment makes escalation costly (unclear channel, undefined expectation, perceived as career risk), escalation will not happen regardless of how many times Tim says it is required.

**The gaps they would identify:**

- **Escalation pathway friction.** Tim says escalate early. But what is the actual mechanism? Is there a defined channel that is low-friction and socially safe? Or must the escalator navigate an informal political decision about who to tell and how? Structural ability means reducing that friction to near zero.

- **Plan of record accessibility.** A single plan of record only functions as a Source 6 tool if it is genuinely the single source and genuinely accessible to everyone who needs it. If the plan lives in a system that requires navigation or permission, it will not be consulted at the moment of decision.

- **AI tool availability.** The AWARE framework correctly identifies architecture as a prerequisite for AI adoption. Source 6 operationalizes this: is the AI tool available at the exact moment the developer needs it, with zero friction? Or does accessing it require a setup step, a license request, a context switch? Environmental design for AI adoption means reducing that friction to zero at the point of use.

## 8. Their Core Critique of Tim's Model

The Influencer authors would summarize Tim's change program as:

**Heavily Source 5, partially Source 6, lightly Source 3, absent in Sources 1, 2, and 4.**

This is a classic enterprise change program shape. It is the shape of programs that produce visible short-term compliance and two-year reversion.

Their specific prediction for Tim's program:

- **Months 1-3:** Named leads take ownership. Bowler charts go live. Weekly evidence reporting begins. Escalations increase.
- **Months 3-6:** Bowler gaming begins. Evidence reporting becomes thinner. Escalations plateau as people learn what triggers consequences.
- **Months 6-12:** Quiet reversion in teams where the informal opinion leaders never changed their behavior. New hires model the pre-change norms because that's what they see around them, not what they read in the manifesto.
- **Month 12+:** The program is declared a success based on compliance metrics. The delivery outcomes have not moved as much as the accountability metrics suggest they should have.

This is not criticism of Tim's intent. It is a prediction based on the empirical pattern the Influencer authors document across dozens of organizational change programs.

## 10. The Bright Spots Gap

One of the Influencer framework's most operationally powerful concepts has no equivalent in Tim's documents: **find the bright spots.**

Bright spots are teams or individuals already exhibiting the vital behaviors and already achieving superior outcomes — inside the current organization, under the same constraints. They are proof that the change is possible without waiting for the transformation program to work.

Tim's framing is primarily prescriptive and top-down. The whole corpus describes what should be. It does not describe who is already doing it, what they do differently, and how that can be spread.

The Influencer strategy:

1. Find two or three mission teams already operating close to the model Tim describes
2. Deeply understand *specifically* what they do differently (not what they say they do — what they actually do, behaviorally)
3. Use those teams as social proof ("this is possible here") and as the first Source 4 teachers for other teams
4. Use the informal opinion leaders in those teams as the Source 3 engine for norm propagation

This strategy costs almost nothing, moves faster than top-down cascade, and produces the lived examples that Source 1 (personal motivation) requires.

## 12. Hypothesized Vital Behaviors for Tim's Program

Based on Tim's diagnostic archaeology — what he must have observed for each prescription to be necessary — two behaviors emerge as candidates.

### Primary vital behavior: Evidence before narrative, in every progress conversation

Not "show evidence at some point." The specific observable sequence: working proof is presented *before* anyone describes what was done.

Why this is the keystone:

Tim's deepest fear is the organization performing transformation without performing it. His most repeated prescription — "narrative without evidence is not progress" — is the diagnostic signature of the exact failure mode he has experienced. He has been in rooms where people said things were on track and then they weren't. Many times.

If this single sequencing behavior changes — if leaders walk into every review and show working proof before they say anything — almost everything else Tim wants cascades naturally:

- You cannot fake the evidence, so the incentive to actually have it goes up
- Blockers surface because the absence of evidence requires an explanation
- The bowler stops being gameable because the data speaks before anyone frames it
- Escalation becomes rational — if you have no evidence to show, you need help
- Meeting quality improves because discussions are about real state, not managed appearances

It is also directly observable. You can watch it happen or not happen in any meeting. That makes it a viable vital behavior in the Influencer sense.

### Precondition vital behavior: Name a human before work begins — not after

Specifically: before any initiative moves past the planning stage, one name is committed to the outcome. Not a team name. A person.

This is the precondition because evidence-first reporting requires knowing who is supposed to produce the evidence. Without a named human, the evidence requirement has no anchor.

### The relationship between them

Naming creates the anchor. Evidence-first is the ongoing behavioral test of whether the anchor is real.

If Tim gets both behaviors reliably, he gets genuine accountability. If he gets only Source 5 mechanisms — bowler charts, escalation paths, earned headcount — without these two behavioral shifts, he gets compliance theater. Which is precisely what he is trying to eliminate.

## Final Observation

Tim has written a delivery model. Influencer would say he has not yet written a change model.

A delivery model describes what the organization should do when it is operating correctly. A change model describes how to get from current behavior to the target behavior, taking into account the full complexity of why people do what they do.

The manifesto, the infinite game doc, the AWARE framework, the senior IC leader model — these are all delivery model documents. They are excellent. They are necessary. They are not sufficient.

The missing document in Tim's corpus is the one that says:

*Here are the two vital behaviors. Here is how we will make them personally meaningful. Here is how we will build the skills to perform them. Here is who the informal opinion leaders are and how we are engaging them. Here is the peer support structure. Here is the structural friction we are removing. Here is the environmental design that makes the right behavior easier than the wrong behavior.*

That document, written at the same quality as the Delivery Manifesto, would complete the change architecture Tim has started.
